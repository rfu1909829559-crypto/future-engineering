# Roadster 2 May Not Be Validating a Flying Car

*Published: October 16 2026*

*Author: Junwei Fu*

---

## Abstract

Tesla's Roadster 2 is often discussed as a potential flying car. This article argues that such a framing may be misleading. Rather than analyzing Roadster as an automotive product, this article examines it as a possible test platform for spacecraft-style maneuvering technologies, including cold-gas thrusters, six-degree-of-freedom control systems, and future orbital mobility platforms.

---

## Introduction

Whenever Tesla's Roadster 2 re-enters the news cycle, the discussion almost always revolves around one question:

> Can it actually fly?

That reaction is understandable.

The most eye-catching aspect of the project is not its acceleration, battery technology, or top speed. It is the possibility that a production vehicle could use a SpaceX-derived thruster system to perform short aerial maneuvers.

![Roadster Demo](../images/roadster-demo.jpg)

*Figure 1. Roadster demonstration. Source: Tesla.*

Recent demonstrations showed the vehicle rotating in mid-air, correcting its attitude, and landing in a controlled manner.

Most people immediately classify this as a "flying car."

I think that might be the wrong question entirely.

The most interesting thing about Roadster 2 may have nothing to do with flying.

---

## This Is Not a Traditional Flying Car

Humanity has been trying to build flying cars for nearly a century.

From the Convair Model 118 to modern eVTOL concepts, most proposals follow the same basic idea:

> Give a car the ability to behave like an aircraft.

Some rely on wings.

![Convair 118](../images/convair118.jpg)

*Figure 2. Convair Model 118.*

Others rely on rotors.

![eVTOL](../images/evtol.jpg)

*Figure 3. Modern eVTOL aircraft.*

All of them ultimately depend on generating aerodynamic lift.

Roadster appears to follow a completely different path.

If Tesla's demonstrations reflect the actual technical direction of the project, the vehicle is not generating lift through wings or rotors.

Instead, it relies on reaction forces generated by multiple thrusters.

In other words:

Roadster resembles a spacecraft more than an aircraft.

According to public information released during Tesla's unveiling event, the Rocket version utilizes approximately ten cold-gas thrusters powered by around 600 liters of compressed gas.

Unlike conventional rocket engines, cold-gas systems do not involve combustion.

They release pressurized gas through nozzles to generate thrust.

This approach is surprisingly common in spacecraft attitude-control systems.

NASA and numerous satellite operators have employed similar systems for decades.

From a technological lineage perspective, Roadster appears much closer to:

- Dragon spacecraft
- Apollo command modules
- Spacecraft reaction control systems

than to:

- Airplanes
- Helicopters
- eVTOL aircraft

That distinction matters.

Because it suggests Roadster belongs to an entirely different engineering family.

## Is Propellant Really the Main Challenge?

Most criticism of the concept focuses on propellant consumption.

That concern is perfectly reasonable.

However, after thinking about it more carefully, I came to a different conclusion:

> Propellant may not be the greatest challenge.
>
> It may actually be evidence that we are evaluating the vehicle in the wrong environment.

Let's perform a simple estimate.

Assume a production Roadster weighs approximately 2,000 kg.

To merely hover, the vehicle must counteract gravity:

$$
F = mg
$$

Where:

$$
m \approx 2000kg
$$

$$
g \approx 9.81m/s^2
$$

Result:

$$
F \approx 19620N
$$

Or roughly:

**20 kN of continuous thrust.**

And that only achieves a stationary hover.

Additional thrust would be required for:

- Acceleration
- Deceleration
- Lateral movement
- Attitude correction
- Disturbance rejection

The question quickly changes from:

> Can it fly?

to:

> How long can it fly?

This is one reason why thrust-based hovering has rarely become the dominant transportation solution.

Airplanes exploit aerodynamic lift.

Helicopters exploit rotating airfoils.

Both take advantage of the atmosphere.

Thrusters continuously consume stored reaction mass.

From an efficiency perspective, they start at a disadvantage.

But what if that disadvantage is telling us something important?

---

## What Happens When Gravity Is No Longer the Problem?

This is where the discussion becomes considerably more interesting.

If a thruster-based mobility system is inherently inefficient in Earth's atmosphere, what environment is it actually optimized for?

Perhaps the answer is:

**Microgravity.**

On Earth:

```text
Thrust
↓
Fight Gravity
↓
Maintain Flight
```

But in orbit:

```text
Thrust
↓
Change State
↓
Perform Maneuver
```

Those two systems may look similar.

Their purpose is completely different.

Consider environments such as:

- Low Earth Orbit
- Lunar Orbit
- Deep-Space Stations
- Lagrange Point Infrastructure

In those settings, vehicles no longer need to continuously generate lift.

Instead, they need to:

- Adjust orientation
- Correct trajectories
- Control velocity
- Manage pointing direction

These are precisely the tasks that thrusters excel at.

In other words:

The very thing that makes Roadster appear inefficient on Earth could become an advantage in space.

---

## Roadster's Ultimate Destination May Not Be Earth

This is admittedly the most speculative section of this article.

Yet it is also the idea I find most interesting.

If Roadster is viewed as a future car, the thruster package appears impractical.

If Roadster is viewed as a flying car, the efficiency argument remains problematic.

But what if Roadster is not primarily validating transportation?

What if it is validating:

- Multi-thruster coordination
- Six-degree-of-freedom control
- Real-time state estimation
- Precision dynamics correction

Viewed through that lens, the project suddenly becomes far more logical.

These capabilities naturally point toward a different class of vehicle:

Not road vehicles.

Not necessarily Mars rovers.

But maneuvering platforms operating in low-gravity or zero-gravity environments.

In such environments, thrusters stop being a way to stay airborne.

They become the movement system itself.

Viewed from that perspective, Roadster begins to resemble something quite different:

Not a future car.

A future maneuvering pod.

Perhaps even an early technology demonstrator for orbital mobility systems that have not yet been built.

---

## The Hard Part Is Probably the Software

Public attention naturally gravitates toward the thrusters.

Engineers are more likely to focus on the control system.

Any free-moving rigid body must manage six degrees of freedom.

### Translational Motion

```text
X
Y
Z
```

### Rotational Motion

```text
Roll
Pitch
Yaw
```

Combined:

```text
6 Degrees of Freedom
```

If Roadster can genuinely stabilize itself in mid-air, orient itself precisely, and land accurately, then its software architecture begins to resemble spacecraft attitude-control systems.

A simplified control loop might look like this:

```text
Sensor Acquisition
↓
State Estimation
↓
Dynamics Calculation
↓
Thrust Allocation
↓
Thruster Control
↓
Feedback Correction
```

All operating within milliseconds.

In that sense, Roadster may have more in common with:

- Falcon 9 landing guidance
- Starship landing systems
- Humanoid robot balancing

than with traditional automobiles.

If the project leaves behind a meaningful legacy, it may not be the thrusters themselves.

It may be the control architecture built around them.

## Science Fiction Has Been Exploring This For Years

At this point I am reminded of a scene from *Mobile Suit Gundam Unicorn*.

During combat, Banshee performs a rapid  thrusters before stabilizing for an attack.

../images/banshee-brake.jpg

*Figure 4. Banshee using attitude-control thrusters to rapidly alter its velocity vector.*

As animation, the sequence lasts only a few seconds.

From an engineering perspective, however, several recognizable operations occur:

1. Cancel existing velocity.
2. Reorient the platform.
3. Construct a stable firing solution.
4. Execute the attack.

Reality is obviously far less dramatic than science fiction.

Yet the maneuver feels believable because it borrows directly from genuine spacecraft control logic.

In space, thrusters are not merely engines.

They are tools for:

- Attitude control
- Pointing control
- Trajectory correction
- Formation maneuvering

Viewed from that perspective, the fascinating aspect of Roadster is not that it may fly.

It is that it may be attempting to bring a fundamentally spacecraft-oriented mode of movement into a terrestrial platform.

---

## The Most Valuable Outcome May Have Nothing To Do With Cars

If this project ultimately leaves behind something important, I doubt it will be a flying automobile.

More likely, it will contribute advances in:

- Compact high-response thrusters
- Coordinated multi-thruster control
- Real-time six-DOF control
- Fault-tolerant maneuvering
- Human-machine safety boundaries

Those same technologies could eventually find applications in:

- Orbital maintenance robots
- Single-person maneuvering vehicles
- Lunar construction equipment
- Deep-space infrastructure
- Large-scale space habitats

Seen from this perspective, Roadster becomes less of a supercar and more of a spacecraft technology demonstrator disguised as one.

---

## Conclusion

Most discussions about Roadster 2 revolve around a single question:

> Can it fly?

I increasingly believe that this is the least interesting question we can ask.

Flight is an outcome.

Validation is the objective.

If Roadster successfully demonstrates a mature multi-thruster control architecture, the knowledge gained may have little to do with the automotive industry.

It may instead belong to future orbital infrastructure, robotic spacecraft, and maneuvering platforms that today still exist largely in the realm of science fiction.

Perhaps the real question Roadster is asking is not:

> How can cars fly?

but:

> How will humans move in a zero-gravity future?

If that interpretation is correct, then Roadster may not be an experimental flying car at all.

It may be an early prototype of a future spacecraft.
